The Park Ridge Loop

Twenty driveways offered as sub work at $60–$100 a visit, salt included. Here is what the route actually looks like, what it costs to run, and what your own price book says these houses are worth.

20 stops · 4 towns Park Ridge · Niles · Mount Prospect · Glenview Prepared 29 Aug 2026
The short version

The route is good. The price band is the problem. Fourteen of the twenty stops sit in a tight Park Ridge–Niles block you could run in about two hours — that is a real route, not a scattering of favors. Your own price book values the full list at $1,415 a push, or $70.75 a house. The offer's midpoint clears that; the offer's floor does not.

At $60 a house you are working 15% below your own retail and carrying the salt. At $80 you are 13% above it. Do not accept a flat blanket rate across all twenty — price them house by house, or set the floor at $70.

The route

One clean loop out of the Des Plaines yard — and one leg that does not belong.

The yard sits almost dead centre of these four towns, which is the single best thing about this offer. Run counter-clockwise and the whole list closes into a loop with no backtracking: Mount Prospect first while the plows are still on the arterials, then east across to Glenview, down through Niles, and finish in the Park Ridge grid closest to home.

Either you commit a second truck to this route, or you negotiate the deadline, or you drop the Glenview and Mount Prospect legs and run Park Ridge–Niles as a single tight 14-stop route in about 4½ hours.

So the offer band brackets your own pricing almost exactly. That is the finding worth sitting with: a subcontract that pays you retail is unusual. Sub work normally runs 50–70% of retail because the general contractor carries sales, billing, and liability. If someone is handing you full retail, the money is being made back somewhere else in the terms — most often in the definition of a "visit" and in who eats the deep-snow events.

The exposure

Every number above is gross margin, before any standby cost and before a dedicated truck. If taking this route means adding a plow truck you would not otherwise run, a ten-event winter at the $60 floor returns about $6,200 against that fixed cost — and Chicago light winters are not rare. The deal is comfortable at $80 and thin at $60. That gap is the whole negotiation.

Before you sign

Ten questions, in the order they can hurt you.

  1. Is $60–$100 per push, or per storm?A 10-inch storm on a 1-inch trigger is three or four pushes.If the rate is per storm event, your margin on the biggest nights goes negative. This is the single most important question on the list.
  2. What happens above 4 and 6 inches?Your own contract doubles at 4–6" and triples at 6+".A flat rate with no depth escalator quietly transfers all deep-snow risk to you.
  3. What is the trigger depth — 1" or 2"?A 1" trigger roughly doubles the number of events versus 2".Cuts both ways: more revenue, but also more nights where you turn out for a light dusting at full cost.
  4. What is the completion deadline?"Clear by 6am" and "within 24 hours of storm end" are different businesses.A hard morning deadline on all twenty forces a second truck. Your standing promise to your own clients is "clear by morning at 2 inches or more."
  5. Who carries slip-and-fall liability?Ask for the indemnity language in writing, plus the certificate of insurance requirements.Ice management liability on twenty properties you do not control is the largest uninsured downside in this deal.
  6. How much sidewalk is included?Front walk only, or the full public sidewalk frontage?Full public frontage on eleven Park Ridge lots is real added time you have not priced.
  7. What are the payment terms, and what is their history?Net-15, net-30, or per-event?Snow costs land in your worst cash months, and your ledger already carries past-due balances across the book. A net-60 general contractor turns a profitable route into a cash problem.
  8. Is it all twenty, or can you cherry-pick?Ask specifically whether you can take Park Ridge and Niles without Glenview.Fourteen tight stops at a good rate beats twenty at a blended rate that includes an outlier leg you already decided to skip.
  9. Is there a non-solicit clause?Can you sell these addresses mowing, cleanups, or aeration in the summer?This is the sleeper value. Twenty warm addresses in a town where you have no snow route at all is worth more than one winter of sub margin — but only if you are allowed to use it.
  10. Is there a page two?The note you sent is marked 1/2.Twenty stops is the analysis above. Thirty changes the crew math entirely.

Recommendation

Take it — as a Park Ridge beachhead, not as twenty favours.

Counter at a per-house sheet with a $70 floor, using the table above as your opening position. It totals $1,470 against their $1,200–$2,000 band, so it sits comfortably inside what they have already said yes to, and it prices the Elmore and Sunset Ridge houses for what they actually are instead of blending them down.

Ask to drop Glenview, or price those two at $80 and $95 specifically. Not because the leg loses money — it does not — but because you made a deliberate call in August to hold Glenview this winter, and two subbed driveways is not a good enough reason to walk it back.

Treat the Mount Prospect four as a freebie you would have taken anyway. 602 S Owen is on Route 6. Those four stops cost you nine minutes of driving and slot into a route you are already building.

And the real reason to say yes: you currently have six snow routes and not one of them touches Park Ridge or Niles. Eleven paid stops inside Park Ridge covers the cost of putting a truck in that town on storm nights. Every retail client you then sell in Park Ridge at your full $45–$80 plus salt is close to pure margin on a truck that is already there. Check the non-solicit language first — but if that door is open, the sub work is the cheapest market-entry you will ever be offered.
